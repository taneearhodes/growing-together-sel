01 · The foundation

Why social and emotional learning matters

Social and emotional learning (SEL) is the process through which children and adults learn to understand and manage emotions, build healthy relationships, show empathy, make responsible decisions, and work toward goals. These abilities support learning because children are better able to participate, communicate, solve problems, and recover from setbacks when they feel safe and connected.

💛

Belonging comes first

Warm, predictable relationships help children feel safe enough to explore, take learning risks, and ask for help.

🧠

Skills can be taught

Emotion words, listening, perspective-taking, and calming strategies develop through practice, feedback, and adult modeling.

🌱

Growth is ongoing

SEL is not a one-time lesson. It grows through everyday routines, play, transitions, conversations, and classroom relationships.

What does the research say? A meta-analysis of 213 school-based SEL programs involving more than 270,000 students found improvements in social-emotional skills, attitudes, behavior, and academic performance (Durlak et al., 2011).
02 · The long view

From prenatal development to adolescence

Development begins before birth. Prenatal health and stress are associated with later differences in children's emotional and behavioral development. A meta-analysis of 74 studies and more than 321,000 parent-child pairs found that prenatal psychological distress was associated with a range of socioemotional outcomes from infancy through adolescence (Phua et al., 2023).

After birth, children learn whether adults are available, whether emotions can be expressed safely, and how relationships work. Responsive caregiving, stable routines, language-rich interactions, play, and supportive learning environments help children practice trust, communication, empathy, and emotional regulation.

These early experiences can influence how adolescents handle stress, form friendships, manage conflict, and respond to challenges. Early adversity may increase risk, but it does not determine a child's future. A study by Rinne et al. (2024) found that sensitive caregiving and emotionally supportive, stimulating home environments moderated associations between prenatal stress and later child behavior.

Educator takeaway: Avoid labeling children or assuming their future is fixed. Consistent, caring relationships and appropriate support can strengthen protective factors at every age.

Prenatal stress is not a parent's fault, and educators should never make assumptions about a child's prenatal history. These findings describe associations and risks—not certainty or destiny.

03 · Developmental snapshots

Three stages, meaningful opportunities

Milestones describe skills many children are developing around a particular age. Children develop at different rates; culture, disability, temperament, language, and experience all matter. Use milestones to guide supportive teaching—not to diagnose or compare children.

👶
Birth–12 months

Infancy

Connection, trust, and shared attention are the beginning of social-emotional learning.

Social-emotional milestones

  • Responds to familiar voices and faces; may smile during social interaction.
  • Enjoys back-and-forth sounds, facial expressions, and simple social games.
  • Uses caregivers as a secure base and may show distress around unfamiliar people or separation.
  • Begins expressing a wider range of emotions and responding to others' emotional cues.

Classroom strategies

1. Serve-and-return interaction: Notice a baby's gaze, sound, or gesture; respond with words, facial expression, or a gentle action. Pause to invite another turn.

2. Songs and face-to-face play: Use peekaboo, name games, mirror play, and gentle songs to build shared attention, joyful connection, and turn-taking.

🧸
3–5 years

Early childhood

Children begin naming feelings, navigating friendships, and practicing self-control with adult support.

Social-emotional milestones

  • Names some feelings in themselves and may begin recognizing feelings in others.
  • Engages in pretend play and increasingly cooperative play with peers.
  • Practices sharing, taking turns, and following simple group expectations.
  • Begins using words to express needs and solve simple conflicts, although adult support is still important.

Classroom strategies

1. Feelings check-in and story talk: Invite children to select an emotion picture and share (or point to) how a character might feel. Ask, “What could help?”

2. Cooperative pretend-play centers: Offer shared roles and materials—restaurant, home, clinic, or building site. Coach children to negotiate roles, request a turn, and repair disagreements.

🎒
6–11 years

Middle childhood

Peer relationships, perspective-taking, confidence, and increasingly independent regulation become more important.

Social-emotional milestones

  • Develops more complex understanding of emotions and can explain some causes of feelings.
  • Builds friendships based on shared interests, trust, and reciprocity.
  • Shows growing empathy and ability to consider another person's perspective.
  • Uses more strategies to manage frustration, follow group rules, and work toward goals—though support is still needed.

Classroom strategies

1. Emotion thermometer or daily check-in: Students privately identify their emotional intensity and choose a matching strategy, such as breathing, movement, drawing, or asking for help.

2. Team challenge and reflection: Use a group building task or problem-solving challenge. Afterwards, reflect: “How did we handle disagreement? Whose idea did we include? What would we try next time?”

These are broad examples, not a checklist that every child must meet on a fixed schedule. Adapt activities for communication differences, sensory needs, culture, and individual strengths.

04 · A skill for every classroom

What is self-regulation?

Self-regulation is the developing ability to notice and manage emotions, attention, behavior, and body responses in ways that fit a situation. It does not mean staying quiet or never becoming upset. It means learning what is happening inside, choosing a helpful response, and returning to learning or connection with support as needed.

🌬️

Example: calming the body

A child feels frustrated when a tower falls, pauses, takes a few slow breaths, and asks for help rebuilding.

🗣️

Example: managing a response

A student feels angry about a game rule, uses words to explain the concern instead of hitting, and listens to a peer's idea.

🎯

Example: shifting attention

A learner notices distraction, checks a visual schedule, and returns to the next step with a reminder.

How teachers can model and encourage it

1
Think aloud.

Model naming your feeling and selecting a strategy: “I feel rushed. I’m going to pause, breathe, and check my plan.”

2
Co-regulate before correcting.

Use a calm voice, get at the child's level, acknowledge the feeling, and offer two safe choices. Teach the skill after the child is ready to engage.

3
Make practice predictable.

Teach and rehearse breathing, movement breaks, help-seeking, and use of a calm space when children are already calm. Use visuals and consistent routines.

Try a simple “Pause–Name–Choose–Reconnect” routine

  1. Pause: Stop and notice body signals.
  2. Name: Identify the feeling or need.
  3. Choose: Select a safe strategy or ask for support.
  4. Reconnect: Return to the activity, repair harm, or make a plan.
Remember: Regulation is taught through relationships. A calm-down corner should never be used as punishment or isolation.
05 · School and home partnership

Small practices families can use at home

Families are children's first teachers. Share practical, respectful ideas that fit each family's routines, language, culture, and resources. Encourage connection rather than perfection.

Infancy · Birth–12 months

Connect through everyday care

  • Respond to coos, cries, gestures, and eye contact with warm attention.
  • Talk, sing, read, and describe everyday routines.
  • Use predictable sleep, feeding, and play routines when possible.
  • Offer comfort during distress; babies learn regulation through caring adults.
Early childhood · 3–5 years

Make feelings part of conversation

  • Name your own feelings and validate the child's: “You seem disappointed.”
  • Read stories and talk about what characters may feel or need.
  • Practice taking turns, asking for help, and solving small problems during play.
  • Create a calm-down kit with books, paper, sensory items, or family-approved comfort objects.
Middle childhood · 6–11 years

Build reflection and independence

  • Use brief daily check-ins: “What felt good today? What was hard?”
  • Practice problem-solving: identify the problem, brainstorm options, consider outcomes, and choose a next step.
  • Model healthy coping such as walking, breathing, journaling, or talking with a trusted person.
  • Notice effort, kindness, and recovery—not only grades or “good behavior.”
Family partnership message: “Children do not need adults who are perfect. They need caring adults who listen, set safe limits, model repair, and keep showing up.”
